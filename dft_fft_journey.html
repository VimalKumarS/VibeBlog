From signal to spectrum.
Building intuition for the DFT & FFT.

Every sound, every image, every vibration is just a sum of pure waves in disguise. The Discrete Fourier Transform is the lens that reveals them. The Fast Fourier Transform is the trick that made it computationally possible. We'll build both from scratch — by hand, with pictures, then with code.

Chapter 01 · Beginner

The world is made of waves.

A sine, a cosine, an amplitude, a phase.

Before we can transform anything, we need to know what we're transforming into. Fourier's grand idea was that any periodic signal — no matter how messy — can be written as a sum of pure sine waves. So sine waves are the alphabet. Let's get fluent.

A pure sine wave has three knobs: its amplitude (how tall), its frequency (how fast it oscillates), and its phase (where it starts). Drag the sliders below and watch the wave breathe.

Fig. 1
A single sine wave
y(t) = A · sin(2π · f · t + φ)

Why sines and cosines?

Because they are the simplest things that oscillate. They are eigenfunctions of differentiation, they are orthogonal to each other, and — most importantly — they are universal building blocks. Pick any signal you like: I can recreate it by stacking enough sines on top of each other. That is the promise. Let's see it happen.

Aha moment Notice how the red sum approaches a clean square shape as you add more harmonics. The little overshoots that never disappear at the edges? That's the famous Gibbs phenomenon — a real artifact of finite-frequency reconstruction.

So if I give you a recipe of frequencies and amplitudes, you can build the signal. But Fourier's truly clever question was the reverse: if I give you the signal, can you find the recipe?

Chapter 03 · Intermediate

The trick is to multiply and add.

How the DFT extracts a single frequency from a signal.

Here's the core insight, and the entire DFT follows from it: if you multiply a signal by a probe wave at frequency f, then add up the result, you get a number that is large only when the signal contains that frequency. Everything else cancels out.

Why? Because two waves at different frequencies are orthogonal — their product averages to zero. But a wave multiplied by itself averages to a positive number. Watch it happen: the green curve below is signal × probe. The teal bar to the right is its sum.

Fig. 3
Probing a 5 Hz signal at different frequencies
sweep the probe — only at f = 5 Hz does the sum light up

The complex exponential — one knob for two waves

To capture both amplitude and phase, the DFT uses the complex exponential e−i2πfn/N, which by Euler's formula is just cos(...) − i·sin(...) bundled together. The cosine extracts the in-phase part, the sine extracts the out-of-phase part, and the imaginary unit i just keeps them in separate bookkeeping columns.

The Discrete Fourier Transform X[k] = Σn=0N−1   x[n] · e−i 2π k n / N x[n] is your signal (N samples). For each frequency bin k = 0, 1, …, N−1, you compute one complex number X[k]. The magnitude |X[k]| tells you "how much" of frequency k is in the signal. The angle tells you the phase.
Chapter 04 · Intermediate

Doing the DFT by hand.

A worked example with N = 4. No magic, just arithmetic.

Let's compute the DFT of the smallest interesting signal: just four samples, x = [1, 2, 3, 4]. The DFT will give us back four complex numbers X[0], X[1], X[2], X[3].

Step 1 — Build the twiddle factors

The exponentials e−i2πkn/N are called twiddle factors. For N = 4 there are only four distinct values: 1, −i, −1, i, and they sit on the unit circle like clock positions.

Fig. 4
The four twiddle factors for N = 4
W4m = e−i 2π m / 4 for m = 0, 1, 2, 3

Step 2 — Apply the formula bin by bin

For each k, sum up x[n] · W4kn. Below is the full computation for our 4-sample signal: 

# Signal:  x = [1, 2, 3, 4],  N = 4
# Twiddles: W^0=1,  W^1=-i,  W^2=-1,  W^3=i  (powers cycle mod 4)

X[0] = 1·W^0 + 2·W^0 + 3·W^0 + 4·W^0
       = 1·1  + 2·1  + 3·1  + 4·1
       = 10 + 0i     # DC: the average × N

X[1] = 1·W^0 + 2·W^1 + 3·W^2 + 4·W^3
       = 1·1  + 2·(-i) + 3·(-1) + 4·(i)
       = -2 + 2i

X[2] = 1·W^0 + 2·W^2 + 3·W^4 + 4·W^6
       = 1·1  + 2·(-1) + 3·(1)  + 4·(-1)   # W^4=W^0, W^6=W^2
       = -2 + 0i

X[3] = 1·W^0 + 2·W^3 + 3·W^6 + 4·W^9
       = 1·1  + 2·(i)  + 3·(-1) + 4·(-i)   # W^9=W^1
       = -2 - 2i
Sanity check Notice X[3] is the complex conjugate of X[1]. This is always true for real-valued input — half of the spectrum is redundant. That's why FFT libraries often return only N/2 + 1 bins for real signals (the rfft).

Step 3 — Read off magnitudes

The magnitude of each complex output tells you the strength of that frequency bin: 

|X[0]| = 10           # DC component (average × N)
|X[1]| = √(4+4) ≈ 2.83
|X[2]| = 2            # Nyquist component
|X[3]| = √(4+4) ≈ 2.83  # mirror of |X[1]|

That's it. That's the entire DFT. It is just "multiply by twiddle factors and sum", repeated once per frequency bin. The conceptual difficulty is finished. What remains is speed.

Chapter 06 · Advanced

The DFT is too slow.

N² gets ugly very fast.

Look back at the DFT formula: for each of N output bins, you sum N products. That's N × N = N² complex multiplications. Tiny for N = 4. Catastrophic for real audio.

N (samples)DFT (N²)FFT (N log₂N)Speedup
864242.7×
1,024~1.05 M10,240~100×
1,048,576 (2²⁰)~1.1 trillion~21 M~52,000×

A one-megapoint DFT on a modern CPU would take minutes. The FFT does it in milliseconds. This single algorithmic change — discovered by Cooley and Tukey in 1965, though Gauss had it scribbled in a notebook around 1805 — is the reason real-time DSP, MRI, JPEG, MP3, OFDM (your Wi-Fi!), and most of modern signal processing exist at all.

Historical aside Cooley & Tukey did not invent the trick — Gauss had a version of it 160 years earlier while interpolating asteroid orbits. He just never bothered to publish it. The 1965 paper landed at the dawn of digital computing, and so this time the world noticed.
Chapter 07 · Advanced

The FFT is a divide-and-conquer trick.

Split, recurse, combine. Same answer, exponentially faster.

Cooley–Tukey's insight: an N-point DFT can be split into two N/2-point DFTs — one over the even-indexed samples, one over the odd-indexed samples — that are then stitched back together with a single twiddle multiplication.

The Cooley–Tukey decomposition X[k] = E[k] + WNk · O[k]   for k = 0 … N/2 − 1
X[k + N/2] = E[k] − WNk · O[k] Where E[k] is the DFT of even-indexed samples and O[k] is the DFT of odd-indexed samples. Each half is itself split again, and again, until you bottom out at N = 1 (which is trivially its own DFT).

The butterfly diagram

The combine step is called a butterfly because of the X-shape it draws: one pair of inputs becomes one pair of outputs by an add and a subtract with a twiddle in between. Below is the full butterfly graph for an N = 8 FFT — three stages, each doing four butterflies.

Fig. 7
An 8-point FFT butterfly diagram
3 stages · 4 butterflies per stage · log₂(8) = 3 levels

Notice the bit-reversed input order on the left side: 0,4,2,6,1,5,3,7. That's the classic FFT signature — splitting evens from odds repeatedly is exactly equivalent to sorting indices by their bit-reversed value.

The whole algorithm in 16 lines of Python

import numpy as np

def fft(x):
    N = len(x)
    if N == 1:
        return x                              # base case

    # 1. Split: evens and odds
    even = fft(x[0::2])
    odd  = fft(x[1::2])

    # 2. Twiddle factors for this stage
    k = np.arange(N // 2)
    twiddle = np.exp(-2j * np.pi * k / N)

    # 3. Combine via butterflies
    top    = even + twiddle * odd            # first half of X
    bottom = even - twiddle * odd            # second half of X

    return np.concatenate([top, bottom])

# Test it on our hand-worked example:
print(fft(np.array([1,2,3,4], dtype=complex)))
# [10+0j,  -2+2j,  -2+0j,  -2-2j]   ← matches our by-hand work!
Why it's fast Each level does N work (combine step). There are log₂N levels (because we keep halving). Total work: N · log₂N. Production FFTs (FFTW, MKL, cuFFT) add cache-aware tiling, radix-4/8 stages, SIMD vectorization, and split-radix butterflies — but the recursion above captures the entire idea.

What you've built intuition for

  • Sines and cosines are universal building blocks. Stack enough of them and you can recreate any signal.
  • The DFT is just "multiply by a probe wave and sum" — repeated once per frequency bin, using the complex exponential to capture amplitude and phase together.
  • The DFT is conceptually simple but computationally O(N²). Not viable beyond a few hundred samples.
  • The FFT is a divide-and-conquer reorganization of the DFT that brings the cost down to O(N log N) — by splitting evens from odds, recursing, and combining with butterflies.
  • Same answer, log-scale faster. That single fact powers nearly every form of modern digital signal processing.
